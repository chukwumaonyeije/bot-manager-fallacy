# **Coding the Coder: Why Physicians Must Understand Their Own Algorithms**

*By Dr. Chukwuma Onyeije | Maternal–Fetal Medicine  
Founder, CodeCraftMD and Doctors Who Code*

---

## **Introduction: When the Tools Start Thinking for Us**

Medicine is shifting into an era where the tools we use no longer simply assist us — they *reason* alongside us.

Clinical models now:

* generate differentials
* recommend diagnostics
* summarize encounters
* extract billing codes
* assess risk
* triage symptoms
* and justify their conclusions

This is no longer a world of static calculators and decision trees.
These systems **interpret**, **predict**, and **suggest**.

And as algorithms increasingly act as our cognitive partners, physicians face a new challenge:

**If we don't understand the logic behind our tools, we will lose control over our workflows.**

This is why physicians must learn to "code the coder" — not by writing software full-time, but by understanding how algorithmic reasoning works, where it fails, and how to direct it safely.

Clinical judgment cannot be replaced.
But it absolutely can be overshadowed if we stop understanding the systems that influence it.

---

## **Why Physicians Can't Afford to Be Passive Users Anymore**

For years, medicine has relied on tools we understood at a surface level:

* calculators
* growth curves
* scoring systems
* imaging protocols
* risk ratios

We knew the inputs.
We knew the outputs.
And we understood how those outputs were generated.

AI breaks that model.

These systems:

* learn from large datasets
* adjust their own patterns
* surface associations invisible to humans
* and sometimes make breathtaking leaps — or breathtaking errors

When a model suggests:
"High risk of ectopic pregnancy,"
or
"Recommend induction for maternal safety,"
or
"Code 99214 based on risk and complexity,"

the physician must be able to ask:

* *Why did it reach that conclusion?*
* *Did it overfit?*
* *Did it miss a key symptom?*
* *Did biased data shape the output?*
* *Did the prompt steer the model unfairly?*
* *Is the reasoning correct, or merely confident?*

If we cannot interrogate the system, we cannot trust it.
If we cannot trust it, we cannot use it.
And if we cannot use it, we fall behind our peers who can.

---

## **The New Clinical Skill: Algorithmic Literacy**

Algorithmic literacy is not coding for coding's sake.
It is the modern form of clinical reasoning.

It includes understanding:

### **1. How LLMs Think**

LLMs don't "know."
They *predict* the next likely word based on patterns.

Understanding:

* hallucinations
* context windows
* input sensitivity
* prompt design
* chain-of-thought reasoning
* reasoning shortcuts

…means you can judge when the model is being insightful vs. when it's being confidently wrong.

### **2. How Data Shapes Output**

Models are only as good as their training data.
If the data includes:

* bias
* gaps
* outliers
* overrepresentation

…then the model will reflect those flaws.

### **3. How Clinical Reasoning Pipelines Work**

Modern AI tools don't just output text.
They run through layers:

* speech-to-text
* summarization
* structured reasoning
* clinical logic
* billing extraction
* EMR formatting

Any layer can fail.
Clinicians must know where the weak links are.

### **4. How to Identify Algorithmic Drift**

Models change.
Sometimes quietly.
Knowing how to detect drift protects both clinicians and patients.

### **5. How to Debug Clinical Workflows**

If a tool outputs the wrong ICD-10 or CPT code,
or mis-summarizes a visit,
or omits a critical detail,
the clinician must know how to:

* trace the error
* adjust the prompt
* modify the workflow
* or escalate the issue

This is not IT work.
This is **clinical oversight** in the AI era.

---

## **Why Understanding Algorithms Protects Physician Autonomy**

If we do not understand our tools, someone else will — and they will set the rules.

Understanding algorithms protects:

* clinical decision-making
* liability boundaries
* workflow design
* patient safety
* billing accuracy
* ethical standards

Every time a physician says,
"I don't need to know how it works,"
someone else becomes the decision-maker:

* an administrator
* a vendor
* a technician
* or an insurer

Control shifts to the people who understand the system.

Medicine cannot allow this.

Physicians must reclaim their agency by mastering the logic behind the tools they use.

---

## **From User → Architect: The Physician's Evolving Role**

As documentation, billing, and basic reasoning become automated, the physician's identity evolves.

The future clinician is:

* part diagnostician
* part proceduralist
* part counselor
* part oversight expert
* part workflow designer

A physician does not need to write production-grade code.
But they do need to understand:

* how the algorithm processes the encounter
* where bias may distort the output
* how prompts shape decisions
* how data is interpreted
* how automation affects the legal record
* how to correct or override the model

This is what it means to be the **architect** of your workflow.

Architects don't build every brick.
They design the system.
They approve the blueprint.
They ensure safety.
They ensure integrity.
They ensure the finished product matches the intended purpose.

Physicians must do the same with AI.

## **CodeCraftMD: A Case Study in Physician-Driven Algorithms**

You've seen this firsthand.

When building **CodeCraftMD**, a physician-coded platform:

* you had to define the logic of ICD-10 extraction
* you had to specify how CPT complexity is derived
* you had to set guardrails
* you had to build safety checks
* you had to test for hallucinations
* you had to ensure compliance workflows functioned correctly

The system didn't become clinically accurate by accident.

It became accurate because a physician — you — directed the algorithm.

This is the model for the future.

AI should not interpret clinical reasoning alone.
It must interpret it under physician direction.

---

## **Why This Matters for the Next Generation of Physicians**

Medical students and residents will inherit a different world.

They will need to learn:

* prompt engineering
* algorithm oversight
* data interpretation
* automated documentation workflows
* verification of model reasoning
* safety checks for bias

The next generation must be taught:
"You are not coding machines.
You are coding *care*."

If they are not trained to lead the technology,
they will be led by it.

---

## **The Goal Isn't to Become a Programmer — It's to Become Unreplaceable**

Physicians who understand algorithms will:

* lead system implementation
* shape clinical workflows
* prevent automation errors
* detect unsafe reasoning
* create better prompts
* design safer guardrails
* maintain clinical autonomy

Physicians who do not may find themselves:

* replaced by "AI supervisors"
* marginalized
* boxed into protocol-driven roles
* forced to trust systems they don't understand
* and unable to challenge poor decisions

Understanding algorithms isn't optional.
It is the new seatbelt of clinical practice.

---

## **Conclusion: The Doctor Who Codes Is the Doctor Who Leads**

Coding the coder is not about writing software.
It is about reclaiming control of the clinical future.

If physicians do not understand their tools,
then their tools will redefine their role without them.

But if physicians step forward —
as architects, investigators, overseers, and designers —
then AI becomes a partner, not a threat.

Automation will change the work.
But physicians who understand algorithms will define the work.

The future of medicine belongs to the clinicians who both practice care
**and shape the systems that deliver it.**
